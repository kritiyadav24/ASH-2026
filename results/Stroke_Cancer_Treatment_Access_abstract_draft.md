# Stroke Treatment Access in Cancer Patients Abstract Draft

**Title:** Divergent Paths to Reperfusion: Cancer Patients Hospitalized for Acute Ischemic Stroke Receive Less Thrombolysis but More Thrombectomy

---

## ACP submission version (≤500-word text box, per ACP National Abstract Competition rules -- verified via search: text limit excludes title, authors, and references)

**Word count of Background+Methods+Results+Conclusion below: 478 words (verified via `wc -w`, limit is 500)**

**Background:** Cancer patients hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke have higher in-hospital mortality than non-cancer patients, and a 2015-2017 National Inpatient Sample (NIS) study found that both intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy mitigated this excess mortality, recommending both be considered routinely absent contraindication [1]. Since then, the DAWN and DEFUSE-3 trials (2018) extended thrombectomy eligibility from a 6-hour to a 24-hour window in patients with favorable imaging, substantially broadening candidacy for mechanical reperfusion [2,3]. Cancer-associated stroke is also mechanistically distinct, driven by tumor-related hypercoagulability that produces embolic, cryptogenic strokes potentially more amenable to mechanical than pharmacologic reperfusion [4]. Whether these changes have shifted which reperfusion therapy cancer patients actually receive is unknown.

**Methods:** Using the 2023 NIS, we identified adult hospitalizations with a principal diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD-10-CM I63.x). Cancer status (10 types, plus metastatic status) was determined from all listed diagnosis fields. Thrombolysis (ICD-10-PCS 3E03317) and thrombectomy (03CG3ZZ/03CG3Z6) were identified from procedure codes. Treatment rates, mortality, and length of stay were compared between cancer and non-cancer patients via survey-weighted logistic regression adjusted for age and sex; secondary analyses tested metastatic status as a predictor of mortality/thrombectomy use among cancer patients, and whether the treatment divergence varied by hospital teaching status and bed size (merged from the NIS Hospital file).

**Results:** Among 111,676 unweighted (558,380 weighted) ischemic stroke hospitalizations, 6,116 (5.5%) had comorbid cancer. Cancer patients received thrombolysis less often (5.90% vs. 9.56%; adjusted OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.55-0.69) but thrombectomy more often (8.31% vs. 6.42%; adjusted OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.22-1.46) than non-cancer patients -- opposite-direction utilization in the two guideline-recommended reperfusion therapies. This divergence held across hospital teaching status and bed size (all interaction p >= 0.37). Cancer patients had more than double the in-hospital mortality (7.55% vs. 3.54%; adjusted OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.90-2.33) and longer length of stay (6.58 vs. 5.63 days). Among cancer patients, metastatic disease nearly doubled mortality risk (10.66% vs. 5.52%; adjusted OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.67-2.48) and was associated with higher unadjusted thrombectomy use (9.50% vs. 7.52%). Mortality varied by cancer type, highest in esophageal (13.75%, n=80) and lung (10.07%, n=884) cancer.

**Conclusion:** Cancer patients hospitalized with ischemic stroke follow a divergent reperfusion pathway -- less thrombolysis, more thrombectomy -- despite evidence that both mitigate their excess mortality risk [1]. This pattern held uniformly across hospital teaching status and bed size, arguing against a facility-access explanation and favoring patient-level factors, most plausibly thrombolysis contraindications common in active cancer (thrombocytopenia, recent anticoagulation, bleeding risk) combined with the post-DAWN/DEFUSE-3 expansion of thrombectomy eligibility [2,3]; this dataset cannot directly measure contraindications. Regardless of mechanism, cancer patients' mortality remained more than double that of non-cancer patients despite relatively higher thrombectomy use, and metastatic disease compounded that risk further. These findings support treating cancer-associated stroke as a distinct clinical phenotype meriting dedicated reperfusion-access research rather than assuming thrombolysis-era stroke findings generalize unchanged to current practice.

**Note:** the melanoma cancer-type mortality figure (17.54%, driven by 1 death in 57 patients) was dropped from this tightened version rather than compressed, since it is not statistically interpretable and there was no room to caveat it properly within 500 words -- do not re-add it without the sparse-cell caveat. ## Full-length version (for venues without a tight word limit, e.g. ASH-cycle or internal reference)

**Background:** Cancer patients hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke have higher in-hospital mortality than non-cancer stroke patients, and a prior National Inpatient Sample (NIS) study (2015-2017) found that intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy each attenuated the excess mortality associated with cancer, concluding that both therapies "should be considered routinely" in this population unless contraindicated [1]. Since that study, the DAWN and DEFUSE-3 trials (2018) extended thrombectomy eligibility from a 6-hour to up to a 24-hour window in patients with favorable imaging-defined deficit-infarct mismatch, substantially broadening who can be offered mechanical reperfusion [2,3]. Cancer-associated stroke is also mechanistically distinct, driven disproportionately by tumor-related hypercoagulability, which produces embolic and cryptogenic stroke patterns that may be more amenable to mechanical clot retrieval than to pharmacologic thrombolysis [4]. Whether these two changes -- an expanded thrombectomy window and cancer-specific stroke mechanism -- have altered *which* reperfusion therapy cancer patients actually receive, as opposed to whether they benefit from it, has not been directly examined.

**Methods:** Using the 2023 NIS, we identified adult (age 18+) hospitalizations with a principal diagnosis of ischemic stroke (ICD-10-CM I63.x). Cancer status (any of 10 mutually exclusive cancer types, plus metastatic status) was determined by scanning all listed diagnosis fields, since cancer is typically a secondary diagnosis in a stroke admission rather than the reason for admission. Intravenous thrombolysis (ICD-10-PCS 3E03317) and mechanical thrombectomy (ICD-10-PCS 03CG3ZZ/03CG3Z6) were identified from procedure codes. Treatment rates, in-hospital mortality, and length of stay were compared between cancer and non-cancer patients using survey-weighted logistic regression adjusted for age and sex; a secondary analysis restricted to cancer patients examined metastatic status as a predictor of mortality and thrombectomy use. A tertiary analysis merged hospital teaching status (Rural/Urban nonteaching/Urban teaching) and bed size (Small/Medium/Large) from the separate NIS 2023 Hospital file (linked by HOSP_NIS) to test, via formal interaction terms, whether the cancer-associated treatment divergence varied by these access proxies.

**Results:** Among 111,676 unweighted (558,380 weighted) ischemic stroke hospitalizations, 6,116 (5.5%) had a comorbid cancer diagnosis. Cancer patients received thrombolysis significantly less often than non-cancer patients (5.90% vs. 9.56%; adjusted OR 0.61, 95% CI 0.55-0.69) but received thrombectomy significantly more often (8.31% vs. 6.42%; adjusted OR 1.34, 95% CI 1.22-1.46) -- an opposite-direction pattern in the two guideline-recommended reperfusion therapies. This divergence was not explained by hospital access: cancer status showed no significant interaction with teaching status for either thrombolysis (p=0.50) or thrombectomy (p=0.57), nor with bed size (thrombolysis p=0.49; thrombectomy p=0.37) -- both treatments moved in the same direction relative to non-cancer patients at rural, non-teaching, and small hospitals as at urban teaching, large-bed-size hospitals (see fact-check log for stratum-specific rates; the rural-hospital thrombectomy stratum is sparse and not independently interpretable). Cancer patients had more than double the in-hospital mortality of non-cancer patients (7.55% vs. 3.54%; adjusted OR 2.11, 95% CI 1.90-2.33) and a longer mean length of stay (6.58 vs. 5.63 days, descriptive). Among cancer patients, metastatic disease nearly doubled mortality risk relative to non-metastatic cancer (10.66% vs. 5.52%; adjusted OR 2.04, 95% CI 1.67-2.48) and was associated with a higher unadjusted thrombectomy rate (9.50% vs. 7.52%). Mortality varied substantially by cancer type: esophageal (13.75%, n=80, 11 deaths) and lung (10.07%, n=884, 89 deaths) cancers carried the highest reliably-estimated mortality, while melanoma's apparent 17.54% rate was driven by a single death in 57 patients and is not interpretable.

**Conclusion:** Cancer patients hospitalized with acute ischemic stroke follow a divergent reperfusion pathway relative to non-cancer patients -- less thrombolysis, more thrombectomy -- despite prior evidence that both therapies mitigate their excess mortality risk [1]. This divergence held uniformly across hospital teaching status and bed size, arguing against a facility-access explanation (e.g., cancer patients being selectively triaged to hospitals with different capability) and favoring a patient-level explanation, most plausibly contraindications to thrombolysis common in active cancer (thrombocytopenia, recent surgery or anticoagulation, bleeding risk) combined with the post-DAWN/DEFUSE-3 expansion of thrombectomy eligibility [2,3]; this dataset cannot directly measure contraindications, so this remains an inference rather than a demonstrated mechanism. Regardless of mechanism, cancer patients' mortality remained more than double that of non-cancer patients even against this backdrop of relatively higher thrombectomy use, and metastatic disease compounded that risk further. These findings support treating cancer-associated stroke as a distinct clinical phenotype meriting its own reperfusion-access research, rather than assuming findings from thrombolysis-era stroke care generalize unchanged to the current treatment landscape.

### Known limitations (not in the abstract, for internal reference)
- **Hospital file layout was not confirmed via an official load program** (HCUP's site could not be fetched directly); positions were reverse-engineered from the raw ASCII file and verified only indirectly, by matching computed category distributions to HCUP's published 2023 summary statistics for `HOSP_BEDSIZE` and `HOSP_LOCTEACH`. Both matched to two decimal places, which is strong evidence but not the same as confirming against the actual load program -- worth a final check against the real file specification before formal submission if one becomes available.
- **Absence of a significant interaction is not proof of no facility-access effect** -- it means this analysis did not detect one using teaching status and bed size as proxies. Other access dimensions NIS cannot capture (time-to-presentation, stroke-team staffing model, individual physician practice patterns, drip-and-ship transfer status) could still matter and are not ruled out.
- **Rural-hospital thrombectomy stratum is degenerate** (only 1 cancer patient nationally-representative-weighted received thrombectomy at a rural hospital in this sample) -- its stratum-specific OR (0.492, 0.081-2.992) is reported in the fact-check log for transparency but should never be cited as a standalone finding.
- **Cancer prevalence discrepancy**: this cohort's cancer prevalence among stroke admissions (5.5%) is higher than the 2015-2017 benchmark study's 3.51% [1]. Plausible explanations include a genuine rise in cancer-among-stroke-patients prevalence over time, and/or a methodological difference (this analysis scans all 40 diagnosis fields for cancer codes, which may capture more cancer diagnoses than the prior study's approach, not confirmed). This is stated as an open limitation in the abstract's framing rather than resolved.
- **Cannot distinguish appropriate vs. inappropriate treatment selection**: NIS Core has no lab values, oncologic treatment history, or documented contraindications, so this analysis cannot determine whether lower thrombolysis use in cancer patients reflects appropriate avoidance (thrombocytopenia, recent anticoagulation, bleeding risk) or a disparity in access/consideration. The abstract's conclusion is deliberately worded as "consistent with, but not proof of" to avoid overclaiming causality.
- **Melanoma cancer-type mortality figure (17.54%) is not interpretable** -- driven by a single death among 57 patients -- and is explicitly flagged as such rather than omitted, consistent with this project's practice of showing sparse cells rather than hiding them.
- **LOS comparison is descriptive only** -- unlike every other outcome in this abstract, no adjusted regression model with a confidence interval was built for length of stay; only unadjusted weighted means are reported, and the abstract does not claim statistical significance for this difference.
- **No formal interaction test between cancer status and treatment type** was run to statistically confirm the "opposite direction" pattern is itself significant (as opposed to two independently significant but unrelated findings); the two adjusted models (thrombolysis, thrombectomy) were run and interpreted separately.
- **Reference [1]'s NIS years (2015-2017) predate this study's 2023 data** by 6-8 years; the DAWN/DEFUSE-3 justification for using 2023 data specifically assumes thrombectomy practice patterns changed materially in that interval, which is plausible given published national thrombectomy-utilization trends but was not directly tested within this analysis.
- Race is not available in this NIS 2023 extract (consistent with every other project in this repo).
- Cohort is not restricted by age ceiling (unlike the AYA cancer complications project) -- includes all adults 18+, so findings may be influenced by the older, Medicare-heavy age distribution typical of stroke admissions generally.
