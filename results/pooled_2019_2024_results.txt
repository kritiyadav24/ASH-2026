NHIS 2019-2024: Vaccination Disparities in Leukemia/Lymphoma Survivors
Pooled, survey-weighted results from scripts/nhis_vaccination_disparities.R
run against a real IPUMS NHIS extract (nhis_00004, years 2016-2024,
restricted to 2019-2024 for this analysis).

See README.md "Known issues fixed" and "Caveats" before interpreting.

========== KEY FINDINGS (strongest to weakest) ==========
1. INSURANCE STATUS is the single largest driver of vaccination uptake:
   uninsured survivors were vaccinated at 9.4% vs. 56.3% for insured
   (adjusted OR 5.94, 95% CI 1.42-24.8 for pneumococcal).

2. LEUKEMIA VS. LYMPHOMA: lymphoma survivors have significantly LOWER
   vaccination uptake than leukemia survivors, even after adjusting for
   race, age, sex, insurance, poverty, education, usual source of care,
   and region:
     Pneumococcal: adjusted OR 0.62 (95% CI 0.41-0.92)
     Flu:          adjusted OR 0.63 (95% CI 0.43-0.91)
   Raw (unadjusted) rates: leukemia 59.6% pneumococcal / 66.9% flu vs.
   lymphoma 52.4% pneumococcal / 59.7% flu. This is one of the
   best-powered comparisons in the analysis (346 leukemia-only vs. 706
   lymphoma-only survivors -- far larger than any racial subgroup here),
   and the effect only reaches significance once demographic/SES
   differences between the two groups are adjusted for. A plausible
   explanation: leukemia treatment (e.g., induction/consolidation,
   transplant workups) typically involves more prolonged, intensive
   contact with hematology/oncology than many lymphoma regimens,
   creating more opportunities for vaccination to be offered.

3. Poverty (middle brackets), education, age, sex, and rurality all show
   significant associations with pneumococcal and/or flu vaccination
   (see "PRIMARY/SECONDARY ANALYSIS" below), but effect sizes are
   smaller than insurance and cancer type.

4. RACE/ETHNICITY: no significant differences between White, Black, or
   Asian survivors on either outcome. AIAN and Other/Multiracial
   estimates are NOT usable -- sample sizes (n=6 and n=15) are too
   small, producing degenerate or extremely wide confidence intervals.
   This dataset cannot answer the original racial-disparity research
   question with any precision; a larger or racially-oversampled data
   source would be needed.
